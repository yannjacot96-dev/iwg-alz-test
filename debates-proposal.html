Debates & Controversies

The defining debates around the diagnosis of Alzheimer disease — from the historical evolution of the disease concept, to the IWG / Alzheimer Association divide on the role of biomarkers in cognitively unimpaired individuals.

1

A short history of how we define AD

Understanding why the IWG defends a clinical-biological framework requires understanding how the definition of Alzheimer disease evolved over a century.

From its description by Alois Alzheimer in 1906 until the late 20th century, Alzheimer disease was defined as a clinical-pathological entity: a clinical syndrome confirmed by characteristic neuropathological lesions at autopsy. The NINCDS–ADRDA criteria of 1984 codified this approach — diagnosis during life could only be "probable", with definitive confirmation requiring post-mortem examination.

The development of in-vivo biomarkers — CSF amyloid and tau, amyloid PET, then tau PET — fundamentally changed what was possible. Starting in 2007, the IWG introduced a clinical-biological framework: biomarkers were used as in-vivo surrogates of the underlying neuropathology, allowing diagnosis during life without compromising the historical logic that anchored disease to a clinical phenotype.

In 2018, the NIA–AA proposed a further step — defining AD biologically alone, on the basis of biomarker positivity, without a clinical requirement. The 2024 AA revision confirmed this direction. The IWG response, also in 2024, maintains that this step disconnects the diagnosis from its clinical-pathological logic and creates more problems than it solves.

1906 – 2006

Clinical-Pathological Entity

AD = clinical syndrome + neuropathological lesions confirmed at autopsy. Diagnosis during life is "probable" only.

2007 – 2017

Clinical-Biological Entity

In-vivo biomarkers (CSF, PET) serve as surrogates of neuropathology. AD can be diagnosed during life — but a clinical phenotype is still required.

2018 – present

Biological Entity (AA)
vs.
Clinical-Biological (IWG)

NIA–AA: biomarker positivity alone defines AD. IWG: a clinical phenotype is still required to call it disease.

The IWG is not anti-biomarker

The IWG position preserves the clinical-pathological logic of disease diagnosis: biomarkers are essential tools in the diagnostic workup, but they are interpreted in the context of a clinical phenotype. The disagreement is about whether biomarker positivity, in the absence of any clinical expression, is sufficient to constitute disease.

2

The root of the dispute

Misunderstandings about what the IWG and AA disagree on are common. The actual disagreement is narrow — and consequential.

Not the question

Are biomarkers useful in the diagnosis of Alzheimer disease?

Both the IWG and the AA agree: in-vivo biomarkers of amyloid and tau pathology are powerful, validated diagnostic tools. Both frameworks integrate them into the workup of symptomatic patients.

The actual question

Should biomarker positivity alone be sufficient to define disease in cognitively unimpaired individuals?

AA 2024: Yes — biomarker positivity establishes the diagnosis of AD even without symptoms.
IWG 2024: No — without a clinical phenotype, biomarker positivity defines a state of risk, not disease.

The core point

The dispute is not "are biomarkers useful?" — it is "is biomarker positivity, by itself, the same thing as having a disease?"

3

Not all asymptomatic biomarker profiles are equal

A common confusion is to treat all biomarker-positive cognitively unimpaired individuals as a homogeneous group. The clinical risk varies dramatically depending on the exact biomarker profile.

Quantitative data accumulated over the past decade show that cognitively unimpaired individuals with positive biomarkers are far from a homogeneous group. The risk of short-term clinical progression depends critically on which biomarkers are positive, where, and to what extent.

Low risk Very high risk
Limited short-term risk
A+ · T−

Isolated amyloid positivity

Isolated amyloid positivity or A+T− status is associated with an increased but still limited short-term clinical risk. The majority of these individuals will not develop symptoms within a 5- to 10-year horizon.

Near-deterministic risk
A+ · T+ (neocortical)

Neocortical A+T+ profiles

When tau pathology has spread to neocortical regions in the presence of amyloid positivity, the rate of short-term clinical progression rises sharply. These individuals constitute the presymptomatic AD category in the IWG lexicon.

This biological gradient is precisely what the IWG lexicon captures by distinguishing "asymptomatic at risk for AD" from "presymptomatic AD" — a distinction that has no equivalent in a binary biomarker-positive / biomarker-negative classification.

4

The role of clinical judgment

A purely biological definition removes clinical judgment from the diagnostic process. The IWG argues that judgment is irreducible — and that even the AA 2024 framework implicitly acknowledges this.

A biomarker result on its own does not tell the clinician whether Alzheimer pathology is the cause of a patient's clinical presentation, or merely a copathology coexisting with another disease process. In older adults, amyloid and tau pathologies frequently coexist with α-synuclein, TDP-43, vascular, or other lesions. Determining which pathology is driving the syndrome requires clinical assessment.

Notably, the AA 2024 criteria themselves acknowledge that the clinical use of biomarkers is currently intended primarily for symptomatic individuals — and that clinical assessment remains necessary to determine whether AD is the dominant contributor to the patient's presentation. The IWG simply makes this explicit and anchors it in the diagnostic framework from the outset.

5

Why this is not merely a semantic debate

The IWG / AA disagreement is sometimes dismissed as a debate over terminology. In practice, it has concrete consequences for patients, clinicians, research, and public health.

A purely biological definition of Alzheimer disease, applied broadly in clinical and research settings, has predictable downstream effects in five domains:

👤

Patient understanding

Disclosing an "AD diagnosis" to a cognitively normal biomarker-positive individual generates anxiety, identity disruption, and assumptions of inevitable decline — even though the majority will not develop symptoms in their lifetime.

🩺

Diagnostic errors

A biomarker-only definition risks labelling individuals with incidental amyloid positivity as "having AD" when their cognitive impairment is driven by another condition. The clinical phenotype is what disciplines the diagnosis.

⚖️

Healthcare inequalities

A biomarker-only diagnosis amplifies disparities where clinical expertise is heterogeneous: the careful clinical evaluation needed to interpret biomarkers correctly is not available in all settings. A purely biological label travels poorly across health systems.

💊

Drug development

If biomarker change is treated as equivalent to clinical benefit, trials can be designed and approved without demonstrating that patients feel, function, or live better. This distorts therapeutic development priorities.

📋

Regulatory decisions

Approval pathways that accept biomarker change as a primary endpoint set a precedent that decouples regulatory approval from patient-relevant outcomes — with broad implications well beyond AD.

🔬

Research priorities

A binary biomarker classification masks the heterogeneity of biomarker-positive individuals and obscures the real research questions: which biomarker profiles confer which level of risk, on what timeline, and in whom.

The IWG position is therefore neither nostalgic nor anti-biological. It is a defence of the principle that a disease is more than its biological substrate — and that the clinical, the biological, and the judgment that connects them must remain together at the centre of how Alzheimer disease is defined, diagnosed, and discussed with patients.
